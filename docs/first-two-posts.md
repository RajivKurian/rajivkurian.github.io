## Reference data

In the code repository, `//sfu:blog_examples` exports the base-conversion data.
Run it with that repository's expected conda environment active:

```sh
./scripts/bazel.py run //sfu:blog_examples > /tmp/base-conversion.json
```

The C++ exporter uses MPFR directed bounds to decide FP32 reference outputs.
The grid contains x = -i/8 for i = 0..640, with separate converted arguments for
wide-constant/FP32-product and FP32-constant/FP32-product paths.

`data/base-conversion.json` is the exported dataset;
`data/base-conversion.html` retains the exported inert JSON wrapper from the
initial draft; the revised post uses two static examples and embeds no dataset. The provenance record pins the generator source hash, data hash,
environment identity, target and precision. No code commit is recorded because
the code repository has no commits yet.

## Attention-throughput motivation

The opening now uses the official Llama 3.1 405B and Qwen3-235B-A22B
configurations (both have 128-wide heads) and a peak-rate balance for H100 SXM
and B200. For the full forward pass, QK and PV total 4*M*N*d FLOPs versus M*N
score exponentials; at d=128 this is 512 FLOPs per exponential. A 16,384-token
full-attention head has 268,435,456 scores and 137,438,953,472 matmul FLOPs.

The GPU comparison uses dense FLOPs/SM/cycle and exponential results/SM/cycle
throughout, with separate BF16 and FP8 rows and a demand/capacity ratio. At
d=128, exponential demand is matrix FLOPs divided by 512. Matrix rates:
Hopper 4096/8192; Blackwell and Ultra 8192/16384; Rubin 8192/32768. FP32
exponential capacities are 16, 16, 32, 32 respectively. Sources: FA3/FA4,
NVIDIA architecture posts, Rubin's NVIDIA-contributed Triton implementation
and CUTLASS instruction definitions. This supersedes the mixed per-second and
per-cycle table and its ratio of 4.375 from rounded Rubin product peaks.

## Concrete cost comparison

`//sfu:blog_cost_example` uses the two exported first-row fits to construct every
row, then checks unsigned integer Horner evaluation on all 2^24 fractional
coordinates. Coefficients have 24 fractional bits, retained Horner results 26.
`data/cost-example.json` records widths, counts and error; its provenance file
records the generator hash and scope. The article builds table, multiplier,
adder and retained-data counts step by step, then compares the two options.
It does not combine unlike bit counts into an unexplained scalar score.

Part 2 uses `early_cost.h` to assign a scalar cost in data-register-bit units.
ROM shape determines mux count and selection depth: eliminate the constant
bottom level, count remaining general muxes and shared address complements.
Weights are .8 per mux, .15 per address complement, 1.6 per Booth row position,
4.5 per aligned sum bit including completion/rounding, and 1 per register bit.
They come from the optimized mapped polynomial and a separate 32-bit variable
mux probe; extraction and source identity are retained in
`code/rtl/audits/booth/calibration/weights.json`.

Scores are 1478 for eight quadratics and 2295 for two cubics. The cubic's lookup
saving is outweighed by its extra/wider arithmetic and carried registers.
The published explanation uses no physical-area numbers or named technology.

The exporter also retains the more detailed Booth gate-graph experiment in
`booth_cost.h` for later implementation comparisons. Its compressor schedules,
Brent–Kung implementation and primitive breakdown are not part of the early
article's cost explanation. Source/data hashes, rates and their derivation are
recorded in cost-example.provenance.json.

The fixed-point worked example now labels guard and sticky directly. The prose
shows below-halfway and even/odd tie examples, explains their relationship to
GRS, and separates numerical narrowing from pipeline storage. Booth's Wikipedia
article is the introductory reference; the NYCU repository page for Yeh–Jen
(2000) supplies the detailed parallel multiplier paper.

The measured RTL optimization log lives in the independent code repository at
`rtl/audits/booth/README.md`. Keep its pipeline-cut, reset, adder, row-order and
mapping-flow experiments for Part 4 rather than expanding Part 2's model.

## Part 2 ending

Use the calibrated scalar score to make a choice: eight quadratics cost about
35.6% less than two cubics (the cubic is 55.3% more expensive). Explain where
the difference comes from, then motivate the coefficient/intermediate-width
search in Part 3. Shared multi-function arithmetic gets one brief final note.

The published comparison now shows only final scores, 1478 and 2295. Its lesson
is why smaller cubic ROMs fail to offset wider/additional arithmetic and carried
pipeline data. Detailed resource counts and weighted subtotals remain in the
exported dataset. The remainder of the series uses piecewise quadratics; Part 3
searches table resolution and fixed-point widths for the final accuracy target.

Coefficient-width variation is now a short subsection within hardware cost.
The standalone coefficient-value table, integer-encoding example and bit-strip
figure were removed from the published post. Their exported data/assets remain
available for supporting notes. The retained explanation connects coefficient
range and precision to ROM row width and multiplier operand width.
